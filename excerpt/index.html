The Ashes of Thornhearth

Chapter One · Opening excerpt

Make yourself comfortable.

By the time Madisyn Fyre saw Thornhearth again, the ash was falling uphill.

She stopped on the ridge with one hand locked around her Blackthorn Staff and watched a grey veil lift from the valley road. The wind came hard out of Embercrest Pass, cold enough to sting her eyes and flatten the dead grass around her boots. Loose ash should have come with it. Instead, grain by grain, it slipped the other way. Toward the mountain.

Madisyn stood still long enough to make sure the climb had not put tricks in her sight. Below her, Thornhearth sat where it always had, wedged between the lower shoulder of Embercrest and the dark hemlock forest. Slate roofs. Black stone walls. The narrow silver cut of the creek. No smoke beyond a few weak chimney threads. No orange glare in the trees. No burn scar on the mountainside. Nothing that explained why the road was grey.

“Wonderful,” she muttered.

Her left leg had begun to ache two switchbacks below the ridge. Standing in place sharpened it, so she planted the iron-shod end of her staff between two slick stones and tested the next patch of ground. Firm. She stepped down with her right foot, brought the reinforced left boot after it, and let the brace take the strain without locking her knee. The iron stays pressed along her calf. One leather strap had started rubbing behind the joint. Later.

Warm ash whispered over the toes of her boots. That stopped her again. She crouched carefully, keeping the damaged leg angled forward while she took most of her weight through the staff. A pinch of ash came away between her gloved fingers. It was finer than hearth ash, with none of the little black curls of bark or charcoal that usually survived a wood fire. No greasy soot. No burned-pine smell. She rubbed it between finger and thumb. Warm. The frost underneath had gone soft.

A faint heat answered beneath her right collarbone. Madisyn froze. The scar there lay under wool, leather, and eight years of practiced avoidance, but she knew the sensation too well. Her living fyre had noticed the ash. Worse, it recognized something in it. She closed her fist.

“No.”

The warmth did not disappear. It withdrew a little, like an animal settling beyond reach.

“That includes you.”

No answer, which was preferable to most of the alternatives.

Madisyn straightened in stages. Her hip objected. She waited until the muscle released, then looked down at the village again. The lower millwheel still sagged eastward. The meeting hall still occupied the square as if Thornhearth had built the rest of itself around the need to argue indoors. A red shawl snapped on a wash line behind the baker’s house, violently bright against all that grey.

Eight years, and the place had managed to age without changing. Her gaze found the lane that led behind the wool sheds. She looked away before it reached the house at the end.

The south road curved down through old grazing terraces. She had chosen it instead of the quarry cut, though the quarry path would have saved nearly an hour. It also dropped over loose shale in three steep runs, each one an excellent place to turn a bad step into a swollen knee and three days of regret.

At sixteen, she would have taken it because someone had told her not to. At twenty-four, she had acquired better ways to be difficult.

She adjusted the strap above her knee, shifted her pack higher, and started down. The first boundary wall emerged from the ash half a mile later. It was made of black granite, stacked shoulder-high. The upper stones were covered in pale lichen. Rowan twigs had been tied above the gate with red wool. An old Thornhearth charm. Sickness, envy, ill luck. The sort of protection people trusted most when they had no idea what was wrong. One bundle hung lower than the others. Madisyn stopped beneath it. The twigs had been nailed over two crooked letters scratched into the lintel.

M. F.

She had carved them there with a stolen kitchen knife when she was nine and spent a week denying it while carrying the evidence in her pocket. Madisyn touched neither the charm nor the initials.

“Still subtle, then.”

She ducked under the gate.

End of opening excerpt
